# The Keeper of Gull Island
Author: Albion Readers
Level: B1

## Chapter 1. The Letter

The letter arrived on a grey Tuesday morning in March, when the rain in London had not stopped for three days. Emma Hart found it on the mat by her front door, between a gas bill and an advertisement for a new pizza restaurant. The envelope was thick and cream-coloured, and her name was written on it in careful, old-fashioned handwriting.

She opened it at the kitchen table while her coffee went cold.

"Dear Miss Hart," it began. "I am writing on behalf of the late Mr Walter Pengelly of Gull Island, Cornwall. Mr Pengelly passed away last month at the age of eighty-seven. In his will, he left you his cottage and the old lighthouse on the island. I would be grateful if you could contact my office at your earliest convenience."

Emma read the letter twice. Then she read it a third time, more slowly, because the words did not make sense. She had never heard of Walter Pengelly. She had never been to Cornwall, except once as a child, and all she could remember about that holiday was the taste of ice cream and the sound of seagulls fighting over chips.

She was twenty-nine years old and worked in an office in the City, where she spent her days answering emails about insurance. She lived alone in a small flat above a launderette, and the most exciting thing that usually happened to her was finding a seat on the Tube in the morning.

"There must be some mistake," she said aloud to the empty kitchen.

But there was a phone number at the bottom of the letter, so after work she called it. A man with a soft Cornish accent answered.

"Ah, Miss Hart," he said warmly, as if he had been waiting for her. "I'm so glad you rang. My name is Tom Trevose. I was Walter's solicitor, and his friend for many years."

"I think you've got the wrong person," Emma said. "I didn't know Mr Pengelly."

There was a short pause on the line.

"No," said Mr Trevose. "But he knew you. Or rather, he knew your grandmother. He was very clear about it. The cottage and the lighthouse go to Emma Hart, the granddaughter of Rose Hart. That is you, isn't it?"

Emma's grandmother had died when Emma was twelve. She remembered a small, quiet woman who smelled of lavender and always kept a box of shells on her windowsill. Nobody had ever explained why she loved those shells so much.

"Yes," Emma said slowly. "That's me."

"Then I think," said Mr Trevose, "you had better come to Cornwall."

## Chapter 2. The Island

Two weeks later, Emma stood on a stone harbour wall in a small fishing village called Porthmor, holding a suitcase and feeling slightly foolish. She had taken a week of holiday from work. Her manager had not been pleased, but she had told him it was a family matter, and in a strange way that was true.

The journey had taken most of the day: a train from Paddington to Penzance, then a bus that rattled along narrow lanes between high green hedges. Now the afternoon sun was low over the sea, and the water shone like a sheet of silver.

Out in the bay, about a mile from the shore, she could see a small rocky island. On its highest point stood a white lighthouse, thin and tall, like a candle on a birthday cake.

"That's Gull Island," said a voice behind her.

She turned. A grey-haired man in a dark blue raincoat was smiling at her. He had kind eyes and a face that had spent many years in the wind.

"Mr Trevose?"

"Tom, please. Everybody calls me Tom." He shook her hand. "Welcome to Porthmor. My nephew Daniel will take us across in his boat. The tide is right, so we should go now, before it turns."

Daniel turned out to be a tall young man of about thirty with untidy dark hair and a serious expression. He nodded at Emma but did not say much as he helped her into the small boat. The engine coughed, then started, and they moved out of the harbour.

The sea was calm, but Emma held on tightly to the side of the boat. Gulls circled above them, crying loudly. As they came closer to the island, she saw that the lighthouse was not as white as it had looked from the shore. The paint was peeling, and some of the windows near the top were broken.

"Nobody has lit it for years," Tom explained. "The new automatic light on the headland took over in the nineties. But Walter refused to leave. He said the island was his home, and he looked after the old tower as if it were still working."

There was a little wooden jetty on the sheltered side of the island. Daniel tied up the boat, and they climbed a steep path to a stone cottage that stood beside the lighthouse. It had a green door, a slate roof and a small garden full of wild flowers.

Inside, it was cold but very tidy. There were books everywhere: on shelves, on chairs, in piles on the floor. A large old map of the Cornish coast hung above the fireplace.

On the kitchen table there was a wooden box with a brass lock. A small key lay next to it, and beside the key was an envelope with Emma's name on it.

"Walter asked me to leave this for you," Tom said quietly. "He said you should open it yourself, when you were ready."

## Chapter 3. Walter's Story

That evening, after Tom and Daniel had gone back to the village, Emma lit a fire in the old stove and made herself a cup of tea. The wind had started to blow, and she could hear the waves breaking on the rocks below. She sat at the table and looked at the box for a long time before she picked up the envelope.

Inside there was a letter, several pages long, written in the same careful handwriting she had seen before.

"Dear Emma," it began. "You do not know me, but I have thought about you many times. I knew your grandmother Rose when we were both young. In the summer of 1956, she came to Porthmor to work in her aunt's tea shop. She was nineteen, and I was twenty-one, the son of the lighthouse keeper.

"I will not pretend that I can describe that summer properly. I am an old man, and old men are not good at speaking about their feelings. But I can tell you that we walked along the cliffs every evening, that she laughed louder than anyone I had ever met, and that she collected shells from every beach in the bay.

"At the end of the summer, I asked her to marry me. She said yes. We planned to tell our families in September. But then her father became very ill, and she had to go back to London to look after him. She wrote to me every week, and I wrote to her. Then, in November, her letters stopped.

"I wrote again and again, but I never received an answer. I was proud and young, and I thought she had changed her mind. Later I heard that she had married someone else. I stayed on the island, and in time I became the keeper myself.

"It was only five years ago that I learned the truth. A woman from London came to Porthmor on holiday and visited my lighthouse. Her name was Margaret, and she had been your grandmother's closest friend. She told me that Rose had never stopped writing. But your great-grandfather, who did not want his daughter to marry a poor boy from Cornwall, had taken her letters before they reached the post. And he had kept mine from her too.

"By the time Margaret told me this, Rose had been gone for many years. I could not change the past. But I could make sure that something of that summer stayed in her family. That is why I have left you the island. Please open the box. Everything inside it belonged to her, or was meant for her."

Emma put the letter down. Her hands were shaking. Outside, the wind was getting stronger, and somewhere a loose window was banging against its frame.

She turned the small key in the lock and lifted the lid.

## Chapter 4. The Box

The box was full of letters. There were dozens of them, tied together in bundles with faded blue ribbon. Some were in Walter's handwriting and had never been sent. On each one, he had written the date: December 1956, January 1957, March 1957, and so on, for more than a year.

Under the letters there was a small black-and-white photograph. It showed a young woman sitting on a rock by the sea, laughing, with her hair blowing in the wind. Emma recognised her immediately, even though she had only ever known her grandmother as an old lady. It was Rose.

At the very bottom of the box, wrapped in a piece of soft cloth, was a shell. It was large and pale pink inside, the kind of shell that you can hold to your ear to hear the sound of the sea. A thin silver ring had been tied to it with a piece of string.

Emma sat very still. She thought of the box of shells on her grandmother's windowsill. She thought of how Rose used to pick them up, one by one, and hold them to her ear when she believed nobody was watching. As a child, Emma had thought it was a funny game. Now she understood that her grandmother had been listening to something far away.

She did not sleep much that night. She read the letters by the light of an old lamp until the early hours of the morning. They were not dramatic or poetic. Walter wrote about the weather, about the boats in the harbour, about a seal that came to sleep on the rocks below the lighthouse every afternoon. But in every letter, at the end, he wrote the same sentence: "The light is still on for you."

At about four o'clock, Emma noticed that the wind had dropped. The sea was quiet. She went to the window and looked out at the dark shape of the lighthouse against the sky, and she suddenly felt that she was exactly where she was supposed to be.

## Chapter 5. The Storm

The next day Daniel came back to the island with a box of groceries and a toolbox.

"Uncle Tom thought you might need some food," he said, putting the box on the kitchen table. "And that window upstairs needs fixing before the next storm. There's a bad one coming tomorrow night, according to the forecast."

He was less serious than he had seemed the day before. While he repaired the window, Emma made sandwiches, and they ate them sitting on the wall outside the cottage. She told him about the letters. She had not planned to, but it was easy to talk to him.

"I knew Walter all my life," Daniel said when she had finished. "He taught me to sail. He used to say that a lighthouse is not really for the people who are safe at home. It's for the people who are lost at sea and can't find their way back." He smiled. "I always thought he was talking about boats."

The storm arrived earlier than the forecast had promised. By the afternoon of the next day, the sky had turned the colour of a bruise, and the wind was so strong that Emma could hardly open the cottage door. Daniel had decided to stay on the island to help her, because it was too dangerous to take the boat back across the bay.

At about eight o'clock that evening, Daniel's phone rang. He listened for a moment, and his face became pale.

"It's the coastguard," he said. "A small fishing boat hasn't come back to Porthmor. Two men from the village, Jack Polglase and his son. Their engine has failed somewhere near the rocks on the north side of the bay. The lifeboat is on its way, but the crew can't see them. The automatic light on the headland went out an hour ago. The storm has cut the power along the whole coast."

Emma looked out of the window. The sea was completely black. There was no light anywhere, except for the weak yellow glow of the lamp on the kitchen table.

"Can the old lighthouse still work?" she asked.

Daniel stared at her. "Walter kept the old oil lamp at the top. He cleaned it every week. He used to say you never know." He was already putting on his coat. "But nobody has lit it for thirty years."

"Then it's about time," said Emma.

## Chapter 6. The Light

The stairs inside the lighthouse went round and round, one hundred and twelve stone steps, and the wind screamed through the broken windows as they climbed. At the top, in the small glass room, stood the great lamp, surrounded by thick curved lenses that shone even in the dark.

Daniel found the oil and the matches exactly where Walter had always kept them, in a metal cupboard with a label that said, in neat handwriting, "Just in case." His hands were cold and wet, and the first two matches went out at once. The third one caught.

For a moment, nothing happened. Then the flame grew, and the lenses caught it and threw it out across the water in a long, bright beam. Slowly, the mechanism began to turn, and the light swept across the bay, once, twice, three times, just as it had done for more than a hundred years.

They stood together at the window, watching the beam pass over the black waves. Twenty minutes later, Daniel's phone rang again. He listened, and then he laughed, a loud, tired laugh of relief.

"The lifeboat found them," he said. "Jack and his son are safe. They saw the light and managed to keep the boat away from the rocks until help arrived. The coxswain says it was like someone had switched the light on just for them."

Emma did not say anything. She was thinking of a sentence she had read many times the night before, in an old man's careful handwriting. The light is still on for you.

The storm passed before morning. When the sun came up, the sea was calm and blue, and the whole village seemed to come out to the harbour to look at the old lighthouse. Later that day, Jack Polglase rowed across to the island with a fruit cake that his wife had baked, and he shook Emma's hand for a very long time.

Emma did not go back to London at the end of the week. She telephoned her manager and told him that she would not be returning. Then she telephoned Tom Trevose and asked him how much it would cost to repair a lighthouse.

"Quite a lot," said Tom. "But I think the people of Porthmor might help. And I have a feeling Walter left a little money aside for exactly this purpose."

That summer, the lighthouse on Gull Island was painted white again. Emma opened a small café in the cottage, where visitors could drink tea, eat Cornish pasties and read Walter's letters, which she had framed and hung on the walls. On the windowsill, she placed a large pink shell with a silver ring tied to it.

Daniel came to help with the boat trips from the harbour, and somehow he never quite stopped coming.

And every evening, as the sun went down over the bay, Emma climbed the one hundred and twelve steps to the top of the tower and lit the old lamp, just for an hour. The modern light on the headland did the real work now, of course. But she liked to think that somewhere, someone who had been lost for a long time might look out across the dark water, see the beam, and know that they could still find their way home.
